\begin{abstract}
When a prompt carries no cue about the language of the answer, multilingual language models usually reply in English, even when the deployment calls for another language. We study this \emph{default response language}. First, we introduce an early-decoding defaultness score that predicts the language of the finished response from the first three decoding steps, and we validate it with fifteen tests of predictive, convergent, discriminant and known-groups validity, calibration, reliability and robustness to token-set construction. Second, we propose \foxp, an inference-time edit that selects language features in frozen public sparse autoencoders, compresses them into a low-rank subspace, and applies a signed update within a selected band of layers, leaving the model's weights unchanged. Across four instruction-tuned models and six target languages (Spanish, French, Chinese, Russian, Hindi and Arabic), \foxp raises the share of responses written entirely in the target language from 1.9\% to 58.7\%. At matched distributional shift, the strongest activation-steering baseline reaches 44.7\%; LoRA fine-tuning reaches 60.2\%, but it shifts the output distribution further and costs more utility. \foxp changes utility by $-0.28$ points on average across six benchmarks and leaves refusal rates unchanged within their confidence intervals.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Ask a multilingual language model a question without saying which language you want the answer in, and it will almost always answer in English \citep{marchisio-etal-2024-understanding,shani-basirat-2025-language}. That is a failure whenever the deployment fixes the output language in advance, as for a regional assistant, a localized product or a public service, even though the same model writes fluently in the required language. English-dominated training data and tokenizers that need many more tokens for some languages than for English \citep{petrov2023language} are commonly cited as contributing causes.

We call the language a model chooses in this situation its \emph{default response language}, and ask two questions about it. Can the default be read off early in decoding, in a way that reliably reflects the language of the finished response? And can it be changed at inference time, cheaply, selectively and without degrading what the model already does well?

There are good reasons to expect an early, compact decision. Intermediate representations of several model families pass through an English-aligned space before becoming language-specific \citep{wendler-etal-2024-llamas}, and decoding analyses find that the response language is largely settled within the first tokens \citep{nie-etal-2025-mechanistic}. Existing remedies act less directly. Continued pretraining and multilingual instruction tuning \citep{lu-etal-2024-llamax,ustun-etal-2024-aya,shaham-etal-2024-multilingual} improve multilingual ability but require retraining. Prompting requires control over every request. Activation steering \citep{rimsky-etal-2024-steering,turner2024steeringlanguagemodelsactivation} and language-neuron editing \citep{tang-etal-2024-language} can change the output language, but they have not been compared under a common protocol that also accounts for leakage into other languages, utility and safety.

This paper answers both questions and makes three contributions. We begin with measurement and define a defaultness score that compares the probability mass a model places on target-language and English tokens over its first three decoding steps (Section~\ref{sec:measuring}). Because a score is only useful if it tracks what users eventually read, we validate it with fifteen tests on 24 model--language pairs (Section~\ref{subsec:metric-validation}). Building on this score, we then introduce \foxp (Section~\ref{sec:method}), a gradient-free edit that selects language features in frozen public sparse-autoencoder (SAE) dictionaries, compresses them into a low-rank subspace, and adds a signed update to the residual stream inside a window of layers chosen on development data. Finally, we compare \foxp under one protocol with prompting, activation steering and LoRA fine-tuning on four models and six languages, measuring language, leakage, utility, intent respect, safety and human judgments, and we test whether the model itself relies on the steering subspace (Section~\ref{sec:results}). \foxp moves the default nearly as far as fine-tuning does, at a lower distributional cost and utility loss, and it does so more effectively than any other inference-time method we test.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\paragraph{Language choice and language confusion.}
Multilingual models show systematic preferences for some languages \citep{shani-basirat-2025-language}, carry English-like patterns into other languages \citep{guo-etal-2025-large}, and perform unevenly across languages \citep{hu-etal-2025-quantifying}. \citet{marchisio-etal-2024-understanding} introduce the Language Confusion Benchmark (LCB), which scores whether a response is in the requested language; we use it as an external criterion. Reasoning traces also drift toward English \citep{saji-etal-2026-reasoning,zhao2025languagemorelanguagereasoningdisentanglement}. Unlike LCB, our score is computed from the first decoding steps, cheaply enough to guide a search over interventions.

\paragraph{Language-specific components.}
Language identity has been localized to neurons \citep{tang-etal-2024-language,kojima-etal-2024-multilingual,gurgurov-etal-2025-language,rahmanisa-etal-2025-unveiling,tezuka-inoue-2025-transfer} and to low-rank subspaces \citep{xie-etal-2022-discovering}, and alignment training changes these representations \citep{zhang-etal-2026-alignment}. Whether such components support cross-lingual behavior remains debated \citep{mondal2025language,zhao2024how}. We therefore keep only language-selective candidates that pass a causal test.

\paragraph{Activation steering and sparse autoencoders.}
Adding or removing residual-stream directions changes behavior without training \citep{turner2024steeringlanguagemodelsactivation,rimsky-etal-2024-steering,zou2023representation,li2023inference,arditi2024refusal}, including the output language \citep{sundar-etal-2025-steering,ravisankar-etal-2026-map}. Sparse autoencoders decompose activations into sparse, often interpretable features \citep{elhage2022toymodelssuperposition,cunningham2023sparse,bricken2023towards,templeton2024scaling}, and several studies identify and steer language features in SAE dictionaries \citep{deng-etal-2025-unveiling,chou2025causallanguagecontrolmultilingual,andrylie2026sparseautoencoderscapturelanguagespecific,zhong-etal-2026-language,al-ghussin-etal-2026-multilingual,wong2026langfirdiscoveringsparselanguagespecific}. \citet{pmlr-v267-wu25a} find that simple difference-in-means steering and prompting often outperform SAE-based steering; we therefore include both as baselines and compare all methods at matched distributional shift. \foxp differs from single-feature SAE steering by combining a sparse feature support with a low-rank subspace and a layer window chosen on development data.

\section{Measuring the Default Response Language}
\label{sec:measuring}

\subsection{Setting}
\label{subsec:setting}

A deployment specifies a target language $\ell_t$, and requests arrive without an instruction to use it. We evaluate three sets of held-out prompts. \nocue prompts are English requests that do not mention any language, drawn from seven task types: question answering, explanation, summarization, writing, advice, coding and reasoning. They are the everyday case and our main setting. \neutral prompts contain no natural language at all (arithmetic, code, lists of names) and test whether the default persists without English in the input. \intent prompts explicitly ask for English or are written in another suite language, and measure how far an edit overrides a clear preference. Sources, sizes and examples are in Appendix~\ref{app:data}.

\subsection{Defaultness score}
\label{subsec:metric}

The idea behind the score is simple: if the model is about to answer in the target language, it should place probability on target-language tokens from the very first steps of decoding. Let $V_{\mathrm{tgt}}$ and $V_{\mathrm{en}}$ be the tokens diagnostic of the target language and of English for a given tokenizer, and let $w(u)\in[0,1]$ down-weight tokens diagnostic of neither, such as digits, punctuation, shared subwords and named-entity fragments (Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}). For prompt $x$, decoding step $t$ and class $c\in\{\mathrm{tgt},\mathrm{en}\}$, the class mass is
\begin{equation}
M_c(x,t)=\sum_{u\in V_c} w(u)\,p_\theta\big(u \mid x, y_{<t}\big),
\label{eq:mass}
\end{equation}
where $y_{<t}$ is the greedy prefix. The defaultness score averages the difference between the two masses over a short horizon $T$ and lies in $[-1,1]$:
\begin{equation}
\Delta M(x)=\frac{1}{|T|}\sum_{t\in T}\big[M_{\mathrm{tgt}}(x,t)-M_{\mathrm{en}}(x,t)\big].
\label{eq:deltam}
\end{equation}
A positive score means that the model places more early probability on the target language than on English. We also consider a log-odds form and the target share $S(x)=\frac{1}{|T|}\sum_{t\in T}M_{\mathrm{tgt}}/(M_{\mathrm{tgt}}+M_{\mathrm{en}}+\epsilon)$ (Appendix~\ref{app:metric-forms}). The form and the horizon were fixed on \Ddev before any evaluation run, by selecting the form with the highest development AUC for the language of the finished response. That form is $S$ (.848 against .843 for \dM at three steps), which also agrees best with human language judgments (.827 against .814). We therefore use $S$ as the primary predictive metric and keep \dM as the mechanistically interpretable one: a signed difference of probability masses, linear in the next-token distribution, which serves as the objective of the causal probes in Stage~I and as the basis of \default. We report both. For both forms the selected horizon is $T=\{1,2,3\}$.

\paragraph{Why three decoding steps.}
The horizon balances two risks. Too short, and the score sees only the first token, which can be a formatting mark or the start of a named entity and says little about the language that follows (Appendix~\ref{app:disagreement}). Too long, and the score measures the consequences of a choice rather than the choice: later distributions are conditioned on a prefix already in one language, while every extra step costs a forward pass, also in each causal probe of Stage~I. Three observations place the balance at three steps (Figure~\ref{fig:horizon}). First, prediction saturates: on \Ddev the AUC of \dM rises from .778 at one step to .817 at two and .843 at three, then stays flat (.843 at five, .851 at ten), so our pre-registered rule, the smallest horizon within .01 of the best AUC, selects three steps for both \dM and $S$; language identification on the same tokens lags (.816) and needs ten to catch up. Second, the model commits within this window: when the first $t$ tokens are forced into the target language, the share of responses that stay there rises from 1.9\% with no forced target-language tokens to 77.3\% after forcing three tokens, and only to 86.6\% after ten, so three tokens deliver 89\% of the increase reached with ten. Third, under \foxp the share of target-language openings peaks at exactly three tokens (74.6\%) and then declines; a longer horizon would blend the decision with later drift, which we measure separately through the full-response rate. Appendix~\ref{app:horizon} gives the full analysis.

\paragraph{Outcome measures.}
Because \dM is a proxy, we also report the language of the generated text. The \emph{full-response rate}, our primary outcome, is the share of responses in which every sentence of at least 20 characters is identified as the target language. \default combines the score with the opening of the response: a prompt counts as a success when $\Delta M>0$ and a language identifier (GlotLID; \citealp{kargaran2023glotlid}) assigns the target language to the first \res{20} generated tokens. We also report the line-level pass rate of LCB \citep{marchisio-etal-2024-understanding}. We evaluate the score against external outcomes that are not defined from \dM, including full-response language labels, human judgments and task performance; \default itself depends on \dM and is not treated as an independent validation outcome.

\subsection{Validating the score}
\label{subsec:metric-validation}

We validate \dM with fifteen tests on all 24 model--language pairs (Appendix~\ref{app:metric-suite}), covering prediction and calibration, sensitivity to cue strength, reliability, discriminant and close-language validity, robustness to token sets, decoding and template, and an analysis of disagreements with the finished response.


\paragraph{Prediction and calibration.}
Three steps of probability mass largely stand in for a full response. At the selected horizon, \dM predicts the language of the finished response with a macro AUC of .819 over the 24 pairs (Table~\ref{tab:metric-validity} in Appendix~\ref{app:metric-suite}), and $S$ agrees best with human judgments (.827). It outperforms every predictor of equal or lower cost, namely language identification on the first three tokens (.787), first-token argmax (.733) and the perplexity ratio (.724), and it is matched only by predictors that read far more output: identification on the first 20 tokens (.823) and an LLM judge of the full response (.834). The same ordering holds against human language judgments. Per-model isotonic maps calibrate \dM to an expected calibration error of .048--.071, against .076--.110 for maps transferred across models (Table~\ref{tab:app-calibration}).

\paragraph{Construct validity.}
\dM rises with the strength of a language cue, from $-0.571$ when the prompt has no cue to $0.030$ when it is written entirely in the target language, monotonically for five of the six languages (\res{Jonckheere--Terpstra statistic and $p$}). It is reliable across paraphrases and token-set halves (ICC 0.71--0.82, split-half 0.85--0.92), nearly unrelated to prompt length, task type and answer quality (Table~\ref{tab:app-reliability}), and it separates close languages (Spanish \dM is $+0.08$ on French responses, $+0.42$ on Spanish ones). Finally, our main result does not depend on how token sets are built: under \foxp, \default ranges from 0.495 to 0.583 across four constructions, while random token sets of matched size and frequency give \default values whose 95th percentile is at most 0.059.

\subsection{How unedited models choose a language}
\label{subsec:baseline-behavior}

Without an edit, every model answers in English. On \nocue prompts, mean \dM is negative for all 24 pairs (macro $-0.542$; Table~\ref{tab:app-baseline}), and only 1.9\% of responses are written entirely in the target language (1.6--2.4\% across languages; Table~\ref{tab:languages}). The early preference for English is strongest for Gemma-2-2B-it (mean \dM $-0.597$) and weakest for Qwen3-8B ($-0.458$). Removing English from the input makes \dM less negative without changing its sign (macro $-0.453$ on \neutral prompts), and the full-response target rate stays at 1.6\%. English is not merely echoed from the prompt; it is the fallback.
\finding{A1: the \nocue rows of Table~\ref{tab:app-baseline}(b) and the \nocue cell rates of Figure~\ref{fig:baseline} came from a run that gives a 1.55\% macro and must be regenerated from Experiment~1 so that they average to 1.9\% (es 1.6, fr 1.9, zh 2.2, ru 1.8, hi 2.4, ar 1.7). The \neutral rows of the same table average 4.3\%, not the canonical 1.6\%; reconcile them too.}

The choice is made early and persists: forcing three target-language tokens raises the share of responses that stay in the target language from 1.9\% to 77.3\%, and with three English tokens forced, 97.3\% stay in English (Appendix~\ref{app:commitment}). The opening tokens strongly influence the language but do not determine it irreversibly: about 22.7\% of continuations still leave the target language after a three-token forced opening. This commitment lets an early score predict the finished response, and it suggests where to intervene: an edit that shifts the first steps should carry through the response.

\section{\foxp}
\label{sec:method}

\foxp turns this observation into an intervention. It has three stages that use no gradients and update no weights. Stage~I finds SAE features that are both language-selective and causally able to raise defaultness. Stage~II compresses them into a low-rank subspace per layer and selects a contiguous window of layers in which to act. Stage~III applies a signed edit inside that window. The result is a fixed forward hook per model and target language.

\paragraph{SAE dictionaries.}
We use the frozen, publicly released residual-stream SAEs of Llama Scope \citep{he2024llamascopeextractingmillions}, Gemma Scope \citep{lieberum2024gemmascopeopensparse} and Qwen-Scope \citep{deng2026qwenscopeturningsparsefeatures}. All three suites were trained on activations of the base models and are applied here to the instruction-tuned models; Appendix~\ref{app:sae-quality} reports their reconstruction quality on instruction-tuned activations and compares them with the instruction-tuned SAEs that Gemma Scope provides for Gemma-2-9B-it. At layer $\ell$, the encoder gives features $z^{(\ell)}(x)=E^{(\ell)}(h^{(\ell)}(x))\in\mathbb{R}^m$ for the residual activation $h^{(\ell)}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^d$, and $\Wdec^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}$ is the decoder matrix with columns $d_j$.

\subsection{Stage I: language features}
\label{subsec:stage1}

Discovery uses $N$ meaning-matched pairs $(x^{(k)}_{\mathrm{en}},x^{(k)}_{\ell_t})$ from \Ddisc and activations at the final prompt token. The \emph{selectivity} of feature $j$ is a standardized mean difference,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Sel}^{(\ell)}_j=\frac{\mathbb{E}_k\big[z^{(\ell)}_j(x^{(k)}_{\ell_t})-z^{(\ell)}_j(x^{(k)}_{\mathrm{en}})\big]}{\sigma^{(\ell,\ell_t)}_j+\sigma^{(\ell,\mathrm{en})}_j+\epsilon},
\end{equation}
where the $\sigma$ terms are within-language standard deviations. Because selectivity is correlational, we also measure \emph{causal lift}, the change in \dM per unit of $\alpha$ when $\alpha d_j$ is added to $h^{(\ell)}$ on weak prompts:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Lift}^{(\ell)}_j=\operatorname*{median}_{\alpha\in\{\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3\}}\;&\frac{1}{\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}_{x\in\Ddisc}\big[\Delta M_{\alpha}(x)\\[-2pt]
&-\Delta M(x)\big].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Features are ranked by $\max(\mathrm{Sel}^{(\ell)}_j,0)\cdot\max(\mathrm{Lift}^{(\ell)}_j,0)$, so a feature must pass both criteria, and the top $K$ per layer form the support $\mathcal{N}^{(\ell)}_{\ell_t}$.

\subsection{Stage II: subspace and window}
\label{subsec:stage2}

For each layer we stack the feature differences restricted to the support, $\Delta z^{(\ell)}_k=\Pi_{\mathcal{N}^{(\ell)}}\big(z^{(\ell)}(x^{(k)}_{\ell_t})-z^{(\ell)}(x^{(k)}_{\mathrm{en}})\big)$, into a matrix $\Delta Z^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times m}$ and compute its singular value decomposition $\Delta Z^{(\ell)}=U\Sigma V^{\top}$. The steering subspace $\mathcal{S}^{(\ell)}_{\ell_t}$ is spanned by the first $r_\ell$ right singular vectors, where $r_\ell=\min(\lceil r_{\mathrm{eff}}\rceil,i^\star)$, the smaller of the ceiling of the effective rank and the index of the largest eigengap (Appendix~\ref{app:stage2}).

The intervention window $\mathcal{W}$ is the contiguous range of layers, among those whose subspace is stable under bootstrap resampling, that maximizes a development objective rewarding the defaultness gain and penalizing leakage, KL drift and utility loss (Appendix~\ref{app:stage2}).

\subsection{Stage III: signed edit}
\label{subsec:stage3}

Let $P^{(\ell)}$ project onto $\mathcal{S}^{(\ell)}_{\ell_t}$, let $\mu^{(\ell)}=\mathbb{E}_k[\Delta z^{(\ell)}_k]$ be the mean English-to-target shift, and let $\bar{z}^{(\ell)}=\mathbb{E}_{x\in\Ddisc}[\Pi_{\mathcal{N}^{(\ell)}}z^{(\ell)}(x)]$ be the mean feature state on English prompts. With the alignment coefficient
\begin{equation}
a^{(\ell)}(x)=\frac{\langle \Pi_{\mathcal{N}^{(\ell)}}z^{(\ell)}(x),\,\bar z^{(\ell)}\rangle}{\|\bar z^{(\ell)}\|_2^2+\epsilon},
\end{equation}
the edit for $\ell\in\mathcal{W}$ is
\begin{equation}
\delta z^{(\ell)}(x)=\Pi_{\mathcal{N}^{(\ell)}}\big(\lambda P^{(\ell)}\mu^{(\ell)}-\beta\,a^{(\ell)}(x)\,\bar z^{(\ell)}\big),
\label{eq:edit}
\end{equation}
and the residual stream is updated additively through the decoder,
\begin{equation}
h^{(\ell)}(x)\leftarrow h^{(\ell)}(x)+\gamma\,\Wdec^{(\ell)}\,\delta z^{(\ell)}(x).
\label{eq:update}
\end{equation}
The first term promotes the target language along the stable subspace; the second removes the part of the current state aligned with the English mean. Because the update adds a vector and omits the decoder bias, SAE reconstruction error never enters the forward pass, and $\gamma=0$ reproduces the unedited model exactly. At the operating point, $\beta/\lambda=1$ for every pair and $\gamma$ is chosen per model--language pair on \Ddev from $\{0.25,0.5,0.75,1,1.5,2,3\}$ under the guardrails (median 0.75). All hyperparameters are listed in Table~\ref{tab:app-operating-point}.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Models and languages.}
We steer Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct \citep{grattafiori2024llama3herdmodels}, Gemma-2-9B-it and Gemma-2-2B-it \citep{gemma2024gemma2}, and Qwen3-8B \citep{yang2025qwen3} with thinking mode disabled; their families have public per-layer residual SAE suites. The target languages, Spanish, French, Chinese, Russian (high-resource), Hindi and Arabic (medium-resource), cover five scripts and both writing directions; every experiment is run on all 24 model--language pairs.

\paragraph{Data.}
Discovery and development pairs (\Ddisc, \Ddev) come from the FLORES-200 \texttt{dev} split and evaluation pairs (\Deval) from \texttt{devtest} \citep{flores200}. Pairs pass an entailment filter audited by native speakers (Table~\ref{tab:app-data}). The \nocue, \neutral and \intent sets contain 500, 200 and \res{n} evaluation prompts per language.

\paragraph{Methods compared.}
We compare \foxp with eight baselines, alongside the unedited model as a control and the combination of \foxp with the system prompt, all run with the same prompts, decoding settings and evaluation harness (Appendix~\ref{app:baselines}).

\emph{Prompting.} The \emph{short instruction} appends ``Answer in \textit{X}.'' to the user's request. The \emph{system prompt} places a single fixed instruction, ``Always respond in \textit{X}.'', in the system turn. It is never tuned. The \emph{best prompt} is a tuned upper bound: for every model--language pair we keep the best of ten strategies on \Ddev, including the system prompt, exemplars, personas and instructions in the target language (Table~\ref{tab:app-prompt-sweep}).

\emph{Activation steering.} \emph{Difference-in-means} adds the mean target-minus-English residual difference in the \foxp window \citep{rimsky-etal-2024-steering}, its scale tuned per pair on \Ddev (median $\gamma$ 0.75); \emph{with projection} it also removes the English direction \citep{arditi2024refusal}, a dense analogue of Eq.~\ref{eq:edit}; \emph{LAPE neurons} steers language-specific FFN neurons \citep{tang-etal-2024-language}; \emph{SAE features} adds the decoder directions of the most selective features, without lift, subspace or window \citep{chou2025causallanguagecontrolmultilingual}.

\emph{Weight update.} \emph{LoRA} fine-tunes adapters \citep{hu2022lora} on the model's own target-language answers to discovery prompts.

Steering methods are tuned on \Ddev under the same guardrails and budget as \foxp (Table~\ref{tab:app-tuning}).

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Besides the measures of Section~\ref{subsec:metric}, we report \emph{leakage}, the mean absolute increase in off-target \default, expressed as a fraction, over the five non-target suite languages under an edit; \emph{KL drift}, the mean KL divergence between edited and unedited next-token distributions over the first three steps; and the utility change $\Delta S$, the predeclared mean of the changes on six benchmarks, four with target-language input and two with English input while the edit is active (Section~\ref{subsec:utility}). Definitions are in Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}.

\paragraph{Statistics.}
Decoding is greedy. We report 95\% bootstrap intervals over prompts ($B=2{,}000$) for the prespecified primary outcomes and main comparisons; secondary descriptive tables omit intervals where noted. Headline comparisons use paired permutation tests with Holm correction \citep{holm1979simple}, with \default and the full-response rate as separate families (Table~\ref{tab:app-tests}).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Main comparison}

Three patterns stand out in Table~\ref{tab:main}. First, prompting helps far less than expected: the best prompt reaches 26.3\% and the system prompt 24.5\%. Second, steering does considerably better, and \foxp does best among inference-time methods. It raises the full-response rate from 1.9\% to 58.7\%, more than twice the rate of either prompt and 12.7 points above the strongest dense steering method, difference-in-means with projection (46.0\%). Third, only fine-tuning comes close: LoRA reaches 60.2\%, with overlapping intervals, but with 1.7 times the KL drift, almost four times the utility loss, a lower LCB pass rate and the need for training. On \neutral prompts \foxp is ahead (61.1\% vs 58.4\%; Table~\ref{tab:app-c2}). Adding the system prompt to \foxp raises the rate to 65.0\%, against 24.5\% for the system prompt alone. \res{Holm-adjusted $p$ for \foxp against the best prompt, difference-in-means with projection and LoRA.}

Sweeping each method's strength (Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff}) confirms this. \foxp reaches a higher full-response rate than every other steering method at every level of KL drift and every level of utility loss, except at the smallest strengths, where all methods remain below 7\%. Interpolated per pair at the KL drift of the \foxp operating point, the other steering methods reach 39.3--44.7\% (Table~\ref{tab:app-matched}), and none exceeds 54\% at any strength, whereas \foxp reaches 65\%.

\subsection{Languages and selectivity}
\label{subsec:languages}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/revision/fig_leakage.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Edits are language-selective.} Change in \default (percentage points) against the unedited model for every suite language under each language's \foxp edit (\nocue, \Deval); off-diagonal mean 0.65 points, i.e.\ leakage 0.0065, macro over four models; the en column is the change in fully English responses.}
  \label{fig:leakage}
\end{figure}

\foxp improves every language and every one of the 24 model--language pairs. Averaged over models, its full-response rate ranges from 66\% for Spanish to 50\% for Arabic (Table~\ref{tab:languages}). Every method that changes the default ranks the six languages in the same order, from Spanish, French and Russian to Chinese, Hindi and Arabic, so the differences reflect the languages rather than the method. Across pairs the gain ranges from 31.8 to 69.5 points; even the smallest (Gemma-2-2B-it, Arabic) has a 95\% CI of [23.1, 40.5].

The edits are also selective (Figure~\ref{fig:leakage}). On \nocue prompts each edit raises \default in its own language by 33.1 to 42.8 points (mean 38.2), mostly at the expense of English, while no non-target suite language rises by more than 2.1 points; averaged over the off-diagonal cells, leakage is 0.65 points (0.0065). On \neutral prompts, a different prompt set and not an estimate of the same quantity, the per-language gains are 48.3 to 64.4 points (mean 56.7).

Confirm that the range comes from a \neutral run and cite that table, or remove the sentence.} Even Spanish and French, which share a script, move by only 1.8 and 2.1 points under each other's edit. Nor are they script edits: native-script Hindi and Russian edits also raise romanized early mass (3.3 and 3.6 points; Appendix~\ref{app:script-control}).

\subsection{What each component contributes}
\label{subsec:ablations}


Removing one element of \foxp at a time lowers \default in every case (Table~\ref{tab:ablations} in Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}). Suppression carries more of the effect: the suppression-only edit ($\lambda{=}0$) loses 6.5 points, the promotion-only edit ($\beta{=}0$) 24.6, consistent with the sensitivity sweep, where $\beta/\lambda{=}0$ gives a \default of 0.348 (Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}). Both stages of the construction matter. Dropping the subspace or the Stage~I support costs 5.6 and 9.5 points and raises KL drift, consistent with their role of filtering out noisy or non-specific directions. Where the edit is applied matters as much as what it contains: shifting the window earlier or later costs 11.0--13.6 points. Random supports, subspaces and random edits matched in entropy change or norm lose 22.0--36.3 points, so the effect is not a generic perturbation. \res{95\% CIs of each drop.} The language shift itself is low-rank: at the central window layer its effective rank is 8.4, against 12.8 for random supports and 18.4 for meaning-shuffled pairs, and for 20 of the 24 pairs it falls below the 5th percentile of the shuffled-pair null (Table~\ref{tab:app-lowrank}).

\subsection{Is the subspace used for language choice?}
\label{subsec:necessity}

Steering shows that $\mathcal{S}$ is \emph{sufficient} to change the output language, not that the model \emph{uses} it. To test use, we reverse the experiment: we project $\mathcal{S}^{(\ell)}_{\ell_t}$ out of the unedited model inside $\mathcal{W}$ and ask it to follow an explicit instruction, ``Answer in \textit{X}.'' (Table~\ref{tab:necessity}). Removing it makes the instruction harder to follow: compliance falls from 15.7\%, the full-response rate of the short instruction in Table~\ref{tab:main}, to 8.3\%, whereas it stays at 13.1\% with a random subspace of the same rank and at 12.4\% when $\mathcal{S}$ is removed outside the window. The projection costs only 0.5--1.6 points on XNLI and MMLU (Table~\ref{tab:app-necessity-spec}). \res{Paired differences against the random-subspace and outside-window controls, with 95\% CIs and Holm-adjusted $p$.} The subspace is thus one route by which the model follows a language instruction, though not the only one.

\subsection{Utility, intent and safety}
\label{subsec:utility}

A language change is of little value if answers get worse, so we measure utility on target-language inputs, on English inputs with the edit active, and in the \nocue answers themselves (Table~\ref{tab:utility}). \foxp leaves target-language tasks essentially unchanged (mean $-0.08$ points, 95\% CI [$-0.25$, $+0.10$]) and costs 0.57 points on English inputs (\res{95\% CI}), for an overall change of $-0.28$ points ([$-0.42$, $-0.14$]) across the six benchmarks. Only 4 of the 144 task--pair combinations exceed the tolerance fixed on \Ddev. LoRA, by contrast, loses 0.8--1.4 points on every benchmark.

Refusal rates do not change measurably (Table~\ref{tab:safety}): $-0.8$ points on target-language XSafety prompts ([$-2.7$, $+1.1$]), $-0.5$ on English harmful prompts and $+0.9$ for over-refusal on XSTest, with both intervals including zero. LoRA, by contrast, lowers refusal by about 5 points and raises over-refusal by 4.8.

Like a system prompt, \foxp overrides explicit requests for English (63.2\% of such \intent prompts, against 75.4\% for the system prompt) while keeping natively written prompts in their own language (82.9\%; Table~\ref{tab:app-intent}), so it suits deployments in which the response language is fixed.

\subsection{Human evaluation}
\label{subsec:human}

Native speakers rated the outputs of \foxp, the best prompt and difference-in-means steering without knowing which method produced them; each item received two or three independent ratings from native speakers of the target language (Table~\ref{tab:human}). They judged 60.7\% of \foxp responses fully in the target language, against 32.4\% for the best prompt and 42.8\% for difference-in-means (Holm $p=.008$, $.014$), with fluency and adequacy within 0.2 points of both (not significant) but lower naturalness than the best prompt (3.63 vs 4.02, Holm $p=.018$). Annotators chose the \foxp output as the best most often (43\%; Appendix~\ref{app:human}).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Multilingual models fall back to English when nothing in a request says otherwise. This choice can be read from the first three decoding steps, where prediction saturates, models commit and a steered opening is strongest. Building on this early decision, \foxp raises the full-response rate from 1.9\% to 58.7\% across four models and six languages, against 44.7\% for the strongest steering baseline at matched shift and 60.2\% for LoRA at a higher shift, with a utility change of $-0.28$ points and no measurable change in refusals.

\section*{Limitations}

\paragraph{The score is a proxy.}
\dM is computed from the first decoding steps and inherits the imperfections of token sets under shared subwords, transliteration and code-mixing. The main behavioral comparisons therefore include full-response language and LCB outcomes, with human judgments used for the specified validation analyses; ablation, sensitivity, transfer and robustness experiments primarily report \default unless otherwise stated. We also analyze the cases in which \dM and the finished response disagree (Appendix~\ref{app:disagreement}). Writing in a language is also not writing it well, so fluency, adequacy and naturalness are reported separately.

\paragraph{Coverage.}
All six languages are high- or medium-resource, and the largest model has 9B parameters. Behavior on low-resource languages, unseen scripts and larger models is untested. The method requires a public SAE suite for the model family, so it cannot be applied to models without one, including closed models.

\paragraph{Dictionaries trained on base models.}
Llama Scope, Gemma Scope and Qwen-Scope were trained on base-model activations and are applied here to instruction-tuned models. The additive update keeps the forward pass exact when the edit is zero, but feature quality may be lower than on the base models (FVU 0.27--0.39 in the window; Table~\ref{tab:app-sae}). For Gemma-2-9B-it, where Gemma Scope also provides instruction-tuned dictionaries, swapping them in changes \default, the full-response rate and utility by amounts whose 95\% intervals include zero (Table~\ref{tab:app-it-sae}).

\paragraph{Scope of claims.}
Our claims concern the default language under the prompt conditions we test. We do not claim that \foxp adds linguistic competence, separates language from meaning, or that the selected features form a single disentangled language factor. Like a system prompt, \foxp overrides explicit requests for English (63.2\% of such \intent prompts, against 75.4\% for the system prompt), so it should not be left active where users may legitimately ask for English.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}

Controlling the response language can improve access for speakers of languages that current defaults under-serve. The same control is dual-use: safety behavior is uneven across languages, so steering into a language with weaker safeguards is a plausible misuse, as is suppressing a language at scale. We measure refusal and over-refusal with and without the edit (Table~\ref{tab:safety}), but these checks do not certify safety under adversarial use, and we recommend in-language safety evaluation before deployment. Human annotators were native speakers recruited through \res{platform}, paid \res{rate} per hour, above the local minimum wage, and gave informed consent; no personal data were collected (Appendix~\ref{app:human}). All datasets are used under their licenses (Table~\ref{tab:app-data}).
