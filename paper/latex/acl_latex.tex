\begin{abstract}
When a prompt carries no cue about the language of the answer, multilingual language models usually reply in English, even when the deployment calls for another language. We study this \emph{default response language}. First, we introduce an early-decoding defaultness score that predicts the language of the finished response from the first three decoding steps, and we validate it with fifteen tests of predictive, convergent, discriminant and known-groups validity, calibration, reliability and robustness to token-set construction. Second, we propose \foxp, an inference-time edit that selects language features in frozen public sparse autoencoders, compresses them into a low-rank subspace, and applies a signed update within a selected band of layers, leaving the model's weights unchanged. Across four instruction-tuned models and six target languages (Spanish, French, Chinese, Russian, Hindi and Arabic), \foxp raises the share of responses written entirely in the target language from 1.9\% to 58.7\%. At matched distributional shift, the strongest activation-steering baseline reaches 44.7\%; LoRA fine-tuning reaches 60.2\%, but it shifts the output distribution further, leaks more into other languages and costs more utility. \foxp changes utility by $-0.28$ points on average across six benchmarks and leaves refusal rates unchanged within their confidence intervals. Code and data will be released upon acceptance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Multilingual language models write fluently in many languages, yet when a request carries no explicit language instruction and no lexical or script cue, they tend to answer in English \citep{marchisio-etal-2024-understanding,shani-basirat-2025-language}. This matters whenever the output language is fixed by the deployment rather than by the user's wording: a regional assistant, a localized product, or a public service that must respond in an official language. English-dominated training data and tokenizers that need many more tokens for some languages than for English \citep{petrov2023language} are commonly cited as contributing causes.

We call the language a model chooses in this situation its \emph{default response language}. Controlling it raises two questions. How should the default be measured, so that a measurement taken early in decoding reflects the language of the finished response? And can the default be changed at inference time, cheaply, selectively and without degrading the model?

Prior work points to an early decision. Intermediate representations of several model families pass through an English-aligned space before becoming language-specific \citep{wendler-etal-2024-llamas}, and decoding analyses find that the response language is largely settled within the first tokens \citep{nie-etal-2025-mechanistic}. Existing remedies act less directly. Continued pretraining and multilingual instruction tuning \citep{lu-etal-2024-llamax,ustun-etal-2024-aya,shaham-etal-2024-multilingual} improve multilingual ability but require retraining; prompting requires control over every request; and activation steering \citep{rimsky-etal-2024-steering,turner2024steeringlanguagemodelsactivation} and language-neuron editing \citep{tang-etal-2024-language} can change the output language but have not been compared under a common protocol that also measures leakage into other languages, utility and safety.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{A validated defaultness score.} We define an early-decoding score of the default response language (\S\ref{sec:measuring}) and validate it with fifteen tests on 24 model--language pairs (\S\ref{subsec:metric-validation}, Appendix~\ref{app:metric-suite}).
  \item \textbf{A gradient-free steering method.} \foxp (\S\ref{sec:method}) builds a sparse, low-rank edit from frozen public sparse-autoencoder (SAE) dictionaries and applies it additively inside a selected window of layers.
  \item \textbf{A controlled comparison.} We compare \foxp with prompting, activation steering, neuron and SAE-feature steering, and LoRA fine-tuning on four models and six languages, reporting full-response language, leakage, utility, intent respect, safety and human judgments, together with ablations and a test of whether the model itself uses the steering subspace (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\paragraph{Language choice and language confusion.}
Multilingual models show systematic preferences for some languages \citep{shani-basirat-2025-language}, carry English-like patterns into other languages \citep{guo-etal-2025-large}, and perform unevenly across languages \citep{hu-etal-2025-quantifying}. \citet{marchisio-etal-2024-understanding} introduce the Language Confusion Benchmark (LCB), which scores whether a response is in the requested language; we use it as an external criterion. Reasoning traces of multilingual models also drift toward English \citep{saji-etal-2026-reasoning,zhao2025languagemorelanguagereasoningdisentanglement}. Unlike response-level scores such as LCB, our score is computed from the first decoding steps, before the response exists.

\paragraph{Language-specific components.}
Language identity has been localized to individual neurons \citep{tang-etal-2024-language,kojima-etal-2024-multilingual,gurgurov-etal-2025-language,rahmanisa-etal-2025-unveiling,tezuka-inoue-2025-transfer} and to low-rank subspaces \citep{xie-etal-2022-discovering}, and alignment training changes these representations \citep{zhang-etal-2026-alignment}. Whether such components support cross-lingual behavior remains debated \citep{mondal2025language,zhao2024how}. We treat language-selective components only as candidates and test their effect causally.

\paragraph{Activation steering and sparse autoencoders.}
Adding or removing directions in the residual stream changes model behavior without training \citep{turner2024steeringlanguagemodelsactivation,rimsky-etal-2024-steering,zou2023representation,li2023inference,arditi2024refusal}, including the language of the output \citep{sundar-etal-2025-steering,ravisankar-etal-2026-map}. Sparse autoencoders decompose activations into sparse, often interpretable features \citep{elhage2022toymodelssuperposition,cunningham2023sparse,bricken2023towards,templeton2024scaling}, and several studies identify and steer language features in SAE dictionaries \citep{deng-etal-2025-unveiling,chou2025causallanguagecontrolmultilingual,andrylie2026sparseautoencoderscapturelanguagespecific,zhong-etal-2026-language,al-ghussin-etal-2026-multilingual,wong2026langfirdiscoveringsparselanguagespecific}. \citet{pmlr-v267-wu25a} find that simple difference-in-means steering and prompting often outperform SAE-based steering; we therefore include both as baselines and compare methods at matched distributional shift. \foxp differs from single-feature SAE steering by combining a sparse feature support with a low-rank subspace and a layer window chosen on development data.

\section{Measuring the Default Response Language}
\label{sec:measuring}

\subsection{Setting}
\label{subsec:setting}

A deployment specifies a target language $\ell_t$, and requests arrive without an instruction to use it. We evaluate three prompt conditions, all on held-out prompts. \textbf{C1 (English, no cue)} contains English requests that do not mention any language, drawn from seven task types: question answering, explanation, summarization, writing, advice, coding and reasoning. \textbf{C2 (language-neutral)} contains inputs without natural-language cues, such as arithmetic, code or lists of names. \textbf{C3 (explicit intent)} contains requests that explicitly ask for English or are written in another suite language, and measures whether an edit overrides a clear user intent. C1 is the main setting; sources, sizes and examples are in Appendix~\ref{app:data}.

\subsection{Defaultness score}
\label{subsec:metric}

Let $V_{\mathrm{tgt}}$ and $V_{\mathrm{en}}$ be the tokens diagnostic of the target language and of English for a given tokenizer, and let $w(u)\in[0,1]$ down-weight tokens diagnostic of neither, such as digits, punctuation, shared subwords and named-entity fragments (Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}). For prompt $x$, decoding step $t$ and class $c\in\{\mathrm{tgt},\mathrm{en}\}$, the class mass is
\begin{equation}
M_c(x,t)=\sum_{u\in V_c} w(u)\,p_\theta\big(u \mid x, y_{<t}\big),
\label{eq:mass}
\end{equation}
where $y_{<t}$ is the greedy prefix. The defaultness score averages the difference between the two masses over a short horizon $T$ and lies in $[-1,1]$:
\begin{equation}
\Delta M(x)=\frac{1}{|T|}\sum_{t\in T}\big[M_{\mathrm{tgt}}(x,t)-M_{\mathrm{en}}(x,t)\big].
\label{eq:deltam}
\end{equation}
A positive score means that the model places more early probability on the target language than on English. We also define a log-odds form and a target-share form (Appendix~\ref{app:metric-forms}). The form and the horizon were fixed on \Ddev by predictive validity before any evaluation run; the selected horizon is $T=\{1,2,3\}$.

\paragraph{Outcome measures.}
Because \dM is a proxy, we also measure the language of the generated text. The \emph{full-response rate}, our primary outcome, is the share of responses in which every sentence of at least 20 characters is identified as the target language. \default combines the score with the opening of the response: a prompt counts as a success when $\Delta M>0$ and a language identifier (GlotLID; \citealp{kargaran2023glotlid}) assigns the target language to the first \res{20} generated tokens. We also report the line-level pass rate of LCB \citep{marchisio-etal-2024-understanding}.

\subsection{Validating the score}
\label{subsec:metric-validation}

We validate \dM with fifteen tests (D1--D15) on all 24 model--language pairs (Appendix~\ref{app:metric-suite}). They ask whether the score predicts the finished response and is calibrated (D3--D5), rises with the strength of a language cue (D6), is stable across paraphrases (D7), ignores properties it should not track (D8), separates closely related languages (D9), is robust to how token sets are built (D10--D12), ranks interventions as their outcomes do (D13), is insensitive to decoding and chat template (D14), and when it disagrees with the finished response (D15).

\paragraph{Prediction and calibration.}
At the selected horizon, \dM predicts the language of the finished response with a mean AUC of .819 (Table~\ref{tab:metric-validity}). It outperforms predictors of equal or lower cost, namely language identification on the first three tokens (.787), first-token argmax (.733) and the perplexity ratio (.724), and it is matched only by predictors that read far more output: identification on the first 20 tokens (.823) and an LLM judge of the full response (.834). Against human language judgments, the four strongest predictors keep the same order. Accuracy rises steeply up to three decoding steps and changes little beyond (Appendix~\ref{app:metric-forms}). An isotonic map fitted per model converts \dM into a probability with an expected calibration error of .048--.071; maps transferred across models are worse (.076--.110), so calibration is model-specific (Table~\ref{tab:app-calibration}).

\paragraph{Construct validity.}
\dM rises with the strength of a language cue, from $-0.571$ when the prompt has no cue to $0.030$ when it is written entirely in the target language, monotonically for five of the six languages (\res{Jonckheere--Terpstra statistic and $p$}). It is stable across paraphrases (ICC 0.71--0.82) and across random halves of the token sets (split-half reliability 0.85--0.92), and it is nearly unrelated to prompt length, task type and answer quality (Table~\ref{tab:app-reliability}). It separates closely related languages: Spanish \dM on French responses is $+0.08$, against $+0.42$ on Spanish responses (Appendix~\ref{app:metric-suite}). Finally, the main result does not depend on how token sets are built: the \foxp gain in \default ranges from 0.495 to 0.583 across four constructions, while random token sets of matched size and frequency give gains whose 95th percentile is at most 0.059 (Appendix~\ref{app:metric-suite}).

\subsection{How unedited models choose a language}
\label{subsec:baseline-behavior}

Without an edit, every model answers in English (Table~\ref{tab:app-baseline}). On C1 prompts, mean \dM is negative for all 24 pairs (macro $-0.542$), 81.7--89.1\% of responses are entirely in English, and no pair exceeds 3.9\% of responses in the target language. The default is strongest for Gemma-2-2B-it (mean \dM $-0.597$) and weakest for Qwen3-8B ($-0.458$); among languages, it is strongest for Russian ($-0.607$). Language-neutral inputs (C2) weaken the default without removing it: \dM stays negative for every pair (macro $-0.453$), and the share of English responses falls to 57.9--80.0\%, mostly in favor of Chinese.

The choice is made early and then persists. When the first three tokens of the response are forced into the target language, 77.3\% of responses continue in it to the end; when three English tokens are forced, 97.3\% stay in English (Appendix~\ref{app:commitment}). Early commitment is what allows an early-decoding score to predict the finished response; it does not explain why the models choose English.

\section{\foxp}
\label{sec:method}

\foxp has three stages that use no gradients and update no weights. Stage~I selects SAE features that are both language-selective and causally able to raise defaultness. Stage~II compresses them into a low-rank subspace per layer and selects a contiguous window of layers. Stage~III applies a signed edit inside that window. The result is an artifact $(\mathcal{N}_{\ell_t},\{\mathcal{S}^{(\ell)}_{\ell_t}\},\mathcal{W})$ per model and target language, after which steering is a fixed forward hook.

\paragraph{SAE dictionaries.}
We use the frozen, publicly released residual-stream SAEs of Llama Scope \citep{he2024llamascopeextractingmillions}, Gemma Scope \citep{lieberum2024gemmascopeopensparse} and Qwen-Scope \citep{deng2026qwenscopeturningsparsefeatures}. All three suites were trained on activations of the base models and are applied here to the instruction-tuned models; Appendix~\ref{app:sae-quality} reports their reconstruction quality on instruction-tuned activations and compares them with the instruction-tuned SAEs that Gemma Scope provides for Gemma-2-9B-it. At layer $\ell$, the encoder gives features $z^{(\ell)}(x)=E^{(\ell)}(h^{(\ell)}(x))\in\mathbb{R}^m$ for the residual activation $h^{(\ell)}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^d$, and $\Wdec^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m}$ is the decoder matrix with columns $d_j$.

\subsection{Stage I: language features}
\label{subsec:stage1}

Discovery uses $N$ meaning-matched pairs $(x^{(k)}_{\mathrm{en}},x^{(k)}_{\ell_t})$ from \Ddisc and activations at the final prompt token. The \emph{selectivity} of feature $j$ is a standardized mean difference,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Sel}^{(\ell)}_j=\frac{\mathbb{E}_k\big[z^{(\ell)}_j(x^{(k)}_{\ell_t})-z^{(\ell)}_j(x^{(k)}_{\mathrm{en}})\big]}{\sigma^{(\ell,\ell_t)}_j+\sigma^{(\ell,\mathrm{en})}_j+\epsilon},
\end{equation}
where the $\sigma$ terms are within-language standard deviations. Because selectivity is correlational, we also measure \emph{causal lift}, the change in \dM per unit of $\alpha$ when $\alpha d_j$ is added to $h^{(\ell)}$ on weak prompts:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Lift}^{(\ell)}_j=\operatorname*{median}_{\alpha\in\{\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3\}}\;&\frac{1}{\alpha}\,\mathbb{E}_{x\in\Ddisc}\big[\Delta M_{\alpha}(x)\\[-2pt]
&-\Delta M(x)\big].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Features are ranked by $\max(\mathrm{Sel}^{(\ell)}_j,0)\cdot\max(\mathrm{Lift}^{(\ell)}_j,0)$, so a feature must pass both criteria, and the top $K$ per layer form the support $\mathcal{N}^{(\ell)}_{\ell_t}$.

\subsection{Stage II: subspace and window}
\label{subsec:stage2}

For each layer we stack the feature differences restricted to the support, $\Delta z^{(\ell)}_k=\Pi_{\mathcal{N}^{(\ell)}}\big(z^{(\ell)}(x^{(k)}_{\ell_t})-z^{(\ell)}(x^{(k)}_{\mathrm{en}})\big)$, into a matrix $\Delta Z^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times m}$ and compute its singular value decomposition $\Delta Z^{(\ell)}=U\Sigma V^{\top}$. The steering subspace $\mathcal{S}^{(\ell)}_{\ell_t}$ is spanned by the first $r_\ell$ right singular vectors, where $r_\ell$ is the smaller of the effective rank, rounded up, and the index of the largest eigengap (Appendix~\ref{app:stage2}).

The intervention window $\mathcal{W}$ is the contiguous range of layers, among those whose subspace is stable under bootstrap resampling, that maximizes a development objective rewarding the defaultness gain and penalizing leakage, KL drift and utility loss (Appendix~\ref{app:stage2}).

\subsection{Stage III: signed edit}
\label{subsec:stage3}

Let $P^{(\ell)}$ project onto $\mathcal{S}^{(\ell)}_{\ell_t}$, let $\mu^{(\ell)}=\mathbb{E}_k[\Delta z^{(\ell)}_k]$ be the mean English-to-target shift, and let $\bar{z}^{(\ell)}=\mathbb{E}_{x\in\Ddisc}[\Pi_{\mathcal{N}^{(\ell)}}z^{(\ell)}(x)]$ be the mean feature state on English prompts. With the alignment coefficient
\begin{equation}
a^{(\ell)}(x)=\frac{\langle \Pi_{\mathcal{N}^{(\ell)}}z^{(\ell)}(x),\,\bar z^{(\ell)}\rangle}{\|\bar z^{(\ell)}\|_2^2+\epsilon},
\end{equation}
the edit for $\ell\in\mathcal{W}$ is
\begin{equation}
\delta z^{(\ell)}(x)=\Pi_{\mathcal{N}^{(\ell)}}\big(\lambda P^{(\ell)}\mu^{(\ell)}-\beta\,a^{(\ell)}(x)\,\bar z^{(\ell)}\big),
\label{eq:edit}
\end{equation}
and the residual stream is updated additively through the decoder,
\begin{equation}
h^{(\ell)}(x)\leftarrow h^{(\ell)}(x)+\gamma\,\Wdec^{(\ell)}\,\delta z^{(\ell)}(x).
\label{eq:update}
\end{equation}
The first term promotes the target language along the stable subspace. The second removes the part of the current state that aligns with the English mean, and is therefore smaller for prompts that already lean away from English. Because the update adds a vector and omits the decoder bias, SAE reconstruction error never enters the forward pass, and $\gamma=0$ reproduces the unedited model exactly. The scale $\gamma$ traces a trade-off curve. At the operating point, $\beta/\lambda=1$ for every pair and $\gamma$ is chosen per model--language pair on \Ddev from $\{0.25,0.5,0.75,1,1.5,2,3\}$ under the guardrails (median 0.75). All hyperparameters are listed in Table~\ref{tab:app-operating-point}.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Models and languages.}
We steer Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct \citep{grattafiori2024llama3herdmodels}, Gemma-2-9B-it and Gemma-2-2B-it \citep{gemma2024gemma2}, and Qwen3-8B \citep{yang2025qwen3} with thinking mode disabled; these are the instruction-tuned models whose model families have public per-layer residual SAE suites. The target languages are Spanish, French, Chinese and Russian (high-resource) and Hindi and Arabic (medium-resource), covering five scripts and both writing directions. All six are covered by XNLI, LCB, Belebele, FLORES-200, XL-Sum and XSafety, and every experiment is run on all 24 model--language pairs.

\paragraph{Data.}
Discovery and evaluation pairs come from FLORES-200 \citep{flores200}: \Ddisc and \Ddev from the \texttt{dev} split and \Deval from \texttt{devtest}; sentences used for translation evaluation are excluded from all three. Pairs pass a bidirectional entailment filter, and native speakers audited 100 pairs per language (Table~\ref{tab:app-data}). Conditions C1, C2 and C3 contain 500, 200 and \res{n} evaluation prompts per language.

\paragraph{Methods compared.}
We compare \foxp with eight methods, all run with the same prompts, decoding settings and evaluation harness (Appendix~\ref{app:baselines}).

\emph{Prompting.} The \emph{short instruction} appends ``Answer in \textit{X}.'' to the user's request. The \emph{system prompt} places a single fixed instruction, ``Always respond in \textit{X}.'', in the system turn. It is identical for every model and language, is never tuned, and represents a deployer who controls only the system turn. The \emph{best prompt} is a tuned upper bound on prompting. For every model--language pair we evaluate ten strategies on \Ddev (an instruction before or after the request, an instruction written in the target language, a paraphrase ensemble, one- and three-shot exemplars, a script cue, a persona, the system message above, and a combination) and keep the one with the highest \default (Table~\ref{tab:app-prompt-sweep}). Because the system prompt is one of the ten candidates, the best prompt is at least as strong on \Ddev by construction; unlike the system prompt, it requires tuning for each model and language and editing every user request.

\emph{Activation steering.} \emph{Difference-in-means} steering adds the mean target-minus-English residual difference in the \foxp window \citep{rimsky-etal-2024-steering}; \emph{difference-in-means with projection} also removes the English direction \citep{arditi2024refusal} and is the dense analogue of Eq.~\ref{eq:edit}; \emph{LAPE neurons} steers language-specific FFN neurons with a matched neuron budget \citep{tang-etal-2024-language}; and \emph{SAE features} adds the decoder directions of the most selective features without causal lift, subspace or window search \citep{chou2025causallanguagecontrolmultilingual}.

\emph{Weight update.} \emph{LoRA} fine-tunes adapters \citep{hu2022lora} on the model's own target-language answers to discovery prompts.

We also combine \foxp with the system prompt. The steering methods are tuned on \Ddev under the same guardrails as \foxp and with at least the same development budget (Table~\ref{tab:app-tuning}), and are also compared along their full strength range.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Besides the measures of \S\ref{subsec:metric}, we report \emph{leakage}, the mean increase in \default for the five non-target suite languages under an edit; \emph{KL drift}, the mean KL divergence between edited and unedited next-token distributions over the first three steps; and the utility change $\Delta S$, the mean change over six benchmarks: four with target-language input and two with English input while the edit is active (\S\ref{subsec:utility}). Definitions are in Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}.

\paragraph{Statistics.}
Decoding is greedy unless stated otherwise. Confidence intervals are 95\% percentile bootstrap intervals with the prompt as the resampling unit ($B=2{,}000$). Headline comparisons use paired permutation tests with Holm correction within each table \citep{holm1979simple}; all tests are listed in Table~\ref{tab:app-tests}.

Table~\ref{tab:main} compares all methods at their operating points. \foxp raises the full-response rate from 1.9\% to 58.7\%, more than twice the rate of the best prompt (26.3\%) and the system prompt (24.5\%), and 12.7 points above the strongest dense steering method, difference-in-means with projection (46.0\%). LoRA fine-tuning reaches a similar 60.2\%, with overlapping confidence intervals, but at a higher cost: 1.7 times the KL drift, more than twice the leakage, almost four times the utility loss and a lower LCB pass rate, and it requires training. On language-neutral prompts \foxp is ahead of LoRA (61.1\% vs 58.4\%; Table~\ref{tab:app-c2}). Adding the system prompt to \foxp raises the rate to 65.0\%, against 24.5\% for the system prompt alone. \res{Holm-adjusted $p$ for \foxp against the best prompt, difference-in-means with projection and LoRA.}

The comparison does not hinge on the chosen operating points. Along each method's full strength range (Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff}), \foxp reaches a higher full-response rate than every steering method at every level of KL drift and every level of utility loss, except at the smallest strengths, where all methods remain below 7\%. At the KL drift of the \foxp operating point, the other steering methods reach 39.3--44.7\% (Table~\ref{tab:app-matched}), and none exceeds 54\% at any strength, whereas \foxp reaches 65\%.

\subsection{Languages and selectivity}
\label{subsec:languages}

\foxp improves every language and every model--language pair. Averaged over models, its full-response rate ranges from 66\% for Spanish to 50\% for Arabic (Table~\ref{tab:languages}). Every method that changes the default ranks the six languages in the same order, from Spanish, French and Russian to Chinese, Hindi and Arabic, so the differences reflect the languages rather than the method. Across the 24 pairs the gain ranges from 31.8 to 69.5 points; the smallest, for Gemma-2-2B-it with Arabic, has a 95\% CI of [23.1, 40.5].

The edits are language-selective (Figure~\ref{fig:leakage}). No non-target suite language rises by more than 2.1 points under any edit. Even Spanish and French, which share a script, move by only 1.8 and 2.1 points under each other's edit. Nor are the edits script edits: native-script Hindi and Russian edits also raise the early mass of romanized Hindi and Russian, by 3.3 and 3.6 points, and the supports found from native-script and romanized text overlap more than matched random supports do (Appendix~\ref{app:script-control}).

\subsection{Human evaluation}
\label{subsec:human}

Native speakers rated the outputs of \foxp, the best prompt and difference-in-means steering without knowing which method produced them (Table~\ref{tab:human}b). They judged 60.7\% of \foxp responses to be fully in the target language, against 32.4\% for the best prompt and 42.8\% for difference-in-means (Holm-adjusted $p=.008$ and $.014$). Fluency and adequacy differ from both baselines by at most 0.2 points, and no such difference survives Holm correction. \foxp outputs are, however, less natural than those of the best prompt (3.63 vs 4.02, Holm $p=.018$). Annotators chose the \foxp output as the best of the three most often (43\% of items). Krippendorff's $\alpha$ is .57--.78 for the ratings and .54--.61 for the choice of best output (Appendix~\ref{app:human}).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We studied the language that multilingual models choose when a prompt does not specify one. We introduced an early-decoding defaultness score, validated it on 24 model--language pairs, and proposed \foxp, a gradient-free edit built from public sparse autoencoders. Across four models and six languages, \foxp raises the full-response rate from 1.9\% to 58.7\%, against 44.7\% for the strongest steering baseline at matched distributional shift and 60.2\% for LoRA fine-tuning at a higher shift, with a mean utility change of $-0.28$ points and no measurable change in refusal rates. Projecting the steering subspace out of the unedited model lowers compliance with an explicit language instruction from 14.8\% to 8.3\%, against 13.1\% for a random subspace, so the model uses the subspace as one route, though not the only one, to follow such instructions.

\section*{Limitations}

\paragraph{The score is a proxy.}
\dM is computed from the first decoding steps and inherits the imperfections of token sets under shared subwords, transliteration and code-mixing. We therefore report the full-response rate, the LCB pass rate and human judgments for every headline comparison, and we analyze the cases in which \dM and the finished response disagree (Appendix~\ref{app:disagreement}). Writing in a language is also not writing it well, so fluency, adequacy and naturalness are reported separately.

\paragraph{Coverage.}
All six languages are high- or medium-resource, and the largest model has 9B parameters. Behavior on low-resource languages, unseen scripts and larger models is untested. The method requires a public SAE suite for the model family, so it cannot be applied to models without one, including closed models.

\paragraph{Dictionaries trained on base models.}
Llama Scope, Gemma Scope and Qwen-Scope were trained on base-model activations and are applied here to instruction-tuned models. The additive update keeps the forward pass exact when the edit is zero, but feature quality may be lower than on the base models (FVU 0.27--0.39 in the window; Table~\ref{tab:app-sae}). For Gemma-2-9B-it, where Gemma Scope also provides instruction-tuned dictionaries, swapping them in changes \default, the full-response rate and utility by amounts whose 95\% intervals include zero (Table~\ref{tab:app-it-sae}).

\paragraph{Scope of claims.}
Our claims concern the default language under the prompt conditions we test. We do not claim that \foxp adds linguistic competence, separates language from meaning, or that the selected features form a single disentangled language factor. Like a system prompt, \foxp overrides explicit requests for English (63.2\% of such C3 prompts, against 75.4\% for the system prompt), so it should not be left active where users may legitimately ask for English.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}

Controlling the response language can improve access for speakers of languages that current defaults under-serve. The same control is dual-use: safety behavior is uneven across languages, so steering into a language with weaker safeguards is a plausible misuse, as is suppressing a language at scale. We measure refusal and over-refusal with and without the edit (Table~\ref{tab:safety}), but these checks do not certify safety under adversarial use, and we recommend in-language safety evaluation before deployment. Human annotators were native speakers recruited through \res{platform}, paid \res{rate} per hour, above the local minimum wage, and gave informed consent; no personal data were collected (Appendix~\ref{app:human}). All datasets are used under their licenses (Table~\ref{tab:app-data}).
